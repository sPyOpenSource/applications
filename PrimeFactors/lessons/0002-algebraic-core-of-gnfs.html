The Algebraic Core of the General Number Field Sieve

Lesson 2 · 20 min · Side topic — generalizes QS from ℤ to number fields

The General Number Field Sieve (GNFS) is the fastest known classical algorithm for factoring integers above ≈100 digits. It was responsible for the RSA-240 factorization (2020, 795 bits).

This lesson focuses on one thing: the algebraic machinery that lifts the congruence-of-squares idea from ℤ into a number field. If you understand this, you understand what makes GNFS tick — and why it's faster than QS.

1. Recall: QS in One Diagram

The Quadratic Sieve, in its simplest form:

Pick a_i = ⌈√n⌉ + i.    Compute g(a_i) = a_i² − n.
Find a subset where ∏ g(a_i) is a square in ℤ.
Then (∏ a_i)² ≡ ∏ g(a_i)  (mod n) → factor via gcd.

The numbers g(a_i) are O(√n). For n of 100+ digits, these are enormous — too large to be smooth with useful probability. GNFS fixes this by finding smaller numbers to test for smoothness, using a number field.

2. The Core Algebraic Setup

We pick:

  • An irreducible monic polynomial f(x) ∈ ℤ[x] of degree d
  • An integer m such that f(m) ≡ 0 (mod n)
  • A root α ∈ ℂ of f(x), giving the number field K = ℚ(α)

The integer m and the root α are tied together by the evaluation homomorphism:

φ: ℤ[α] → ℤ_n    φ(α) = m mod n

This is well-defined because f(α) = 0 in ℤ[α], and f(m) ≡ 0 (mod n), so the relation f(α) = 0 maps to the relation f(m) ≡ 0 in ℤ_n.

Why two representations? The element a − bα ∈ ℤ[α] and its image a − bm ∈ ℤ represent the same information in two rings. If we can make both sides squares simultaneously, φ gives us a congruence of squares in ℤ_n.

3. The Norm: The Bridge Between Both Worlds

For an element a − bα ∈ ℤ[α], its norm down to ℚ is:

N(a − bα) = ∏_{σ: K → ℂ} σ(a − bα)
= b^d · f(a/b)   (the resultant formula)

This is the crucial formula. The norm is an integer (since a − bα is an algebraic integer when f is monic), and it's exactly b^d · f(a/b).

This is the whole reason GNFS works. The values we test for smoothness are N(a − bα) = b^d · f(a/b). These are much smaller than √n, because f is chosen (via polynomial selection) to have very small coefficients — typically of size approximately n^{1/d}. By increasing d, we shrink the smoothness candidates. The optimal trade-off gives GNFS its sub-exponential complexity L_n[1/3, (64/9)^{1/3}].

Concretely: for n of ≈100 digits, QS tests numbers of size ≈10^50. GNFS with degree d ≈ 5 tests numbers of size ≈10^20 — vastly more likely to be smooth.

4. The Two-Sided Smoothness Problem

For each pair (a, b) with gcd(a, b) = 1 and b > 0, we get two numbers:

SideValueRing
Rational a − bm ℤ
Algebraic N(a − bα) = b^d · f(a/b) ℤ (the norm)

We need both to be B-smooth simultaneously. This defines our relation: a pair (a, b) where both the rational side and the algebraic norm factor over a chosen factor base.

The rational side factors into ordinary primes. The algebraic side factors into prime ideals in O_K, but the norm's integer factorization tells us which prime ideals appear.

5. From Relations to Congruence of Squares

Suppose we find a set S of (a, b) pairs such that:

(rational)   ∏_{(a,b)∈S} (a − bm) = A²  in ℤ
(algebraic)   ∏_{(a,b)∈S} (a − bα) = β²  in ℤ[α]

Apply φ to the algebraic square:

φ(β)² = φ(β²) = φ(∏(a − bα)) = ∏ φ(a − bα)
 = ∏ (a − bm) ≡ A²  (mod n)

So φ(β)² ≡ A² (mod n). This is our congruence of squares! If φ(β) ≢ ±A (mod n), then gcd(φ(β) − A, n) gives a factor.

The diagram:

  ℤ[α] ──φ──→ ℤ_n
   |            |
   | square     | square
   v            v
  β²  ──→  φ(β²) = A²
The algebraic square root β lives in ℤ[α] (an algebraic integer). Its image under φ is an integer modulo n. That integer and A give us the congruence.

6. The Square Root Problem

The hardest part: finding β ∈ ℤ[α] such that β² = ∏(a − bα). We know the product is a square in ℤ[α], but actually computing its square root requires:

  • Representing the product's prime ideal factorization (obtained during sieving)
  • Finding a representation β ∈ ℤ[α] from the ideal factorization — this is the algebraic square root step, and it's notoriously delicate

Several methods exist: the Chinese remainder theorem approach (Montgomery), the Couveignes method, and the more practical "naive" method using CRT modulo large primes.

7. Complexity Comparison

AlgorithmComplexity (L-notation)Best use
Trial division n^{1/2} < 10^10

Quadratic Sieve L_n[1/2, 1] ~30–100 digits
GNFS L_n[1/3, (64/9)^{1/3}] ~100+ digits

Where L_n[a, c] = exp((c + o(1)) (log n)^a (log log n)^{1-a}). The jump from a = 1/2 to a = 1/3 is what makes GNFS the asymptotic champion: the exponent of log n gets smaller.

Retrieval Practice

Q1. Why does GNFS test smaller numbers for smoothness than QS?

QS tests g(x) ≈ √n. GNFS tests N(a − bα) = b^d f(a/b) ≈ n^{1/d}. By choosing d ≈ (log n)^{1/3}, these values are much smaller.

Q2. What condition must m satisfy?

f(m) ≡ 0 (mod n). This ensures φ(α) = m is a well-defined ring homomorphism from ℤ[α] to ℤ_n.

Q3. A relation (a, b) requires smoothness on two sides. What are they?

Rational side: a − bm. Algebraic side: N(a − bα) = b^d f(a/b). Both must be B-smooth.
